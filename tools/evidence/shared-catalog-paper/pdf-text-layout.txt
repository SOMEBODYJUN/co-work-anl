   GOLDEN-RATIO EQUILIBRIA FOR TWO FACILITIES
             WITH ATOMIC CLIENTS

       Abstract. Krogmann, Lenzner, Skopalik, Uetz, and Vos introduced
       two-stage facility location with atomic clients and left open whether the
       golden ratio guarantees an approximate subgame-perfect equilibrium. We
       settle this question for two facilities with the same finite location catalog.
       For arbitrary positive client weights, we construct a selection of exact
       client Nash equilibria
                          √     supporting a ϕ-approximate facility equilibrium,
       where ϕ = (1 + 5)/2. For rational input given by explicit coverage
       sets, the construction runs in polynomial time and returns rational client
       strategies. The approximation factor matches the known two-facility
       lower bound. The proof uses finite menus of client equilibria and an
       overlap estimate for two selected locations. We show that these local
       choices exclude every closed response cycle that could obstruct the
       required equilibrium.




                                 1. Introduction
   A facility in a two-stage location game chooses its location before clients
decide which facility to use. Its payoff therefore depends on the client
equilibrium selected after the move. Atomic clients may have several Nash
equilibria at a given placement, yielding different facility loads. To establish
subgame-perfect equilibrium, we must choose a facility placement together
with client equilibria at all possible placements.
   Krogmann, Lenzner, Skopalik, Uetz, and Vos [2] introduced this atomic
model. They established exact existence for equal client weights and a golden-
ratio lower bound for weighted clients, leaving the corresponding existence
guarantee open. We prove that the golden ratio suffices when two facilities
share the same finite location set. For rational input, we also construct the
facility placement and its client continuations in polynomial time. Thus the
two-facility common-catalog case attains the known lower bound.
1.1. The model. We use the atomic client model of [2, Section 1.1], with
two facilities and a common location catalog. Describing eligibility by sets
permits the location and client sets to be distinct.
Definition 1.1. An instance consists of a finite client set G, positive weights
wg , a finite nonempty location set S, and coverage sets Cs ⊆ G for s ∈ S.
Both labeled facilities choose a location in S, simultaneously and with co-
location permitted. After a placement (s, t), each covered client chooses one
of its accessible facilities, independently of the other clients. An uncovered
client is not served. A client minimizes its expected realized facility load,
including its own weight; a facility maximizes its expected attracted weight.

1.2. The proof idea and its relation to earlier work. The argument
has two main ingredients. First, we construct finite menus of exact client
equilibria, using the same menus to support a placement and to evaluate
every deviation from it. Minimizing the deviator’s load within these menus
defines a response map on locations. Second, we analyze a closed response
cycle under the assumption that its menus contain no supporting placement.
The overlap inequalities require the pairs of sufficiently heavy clients to form
a star. Two selected locations then bound the common client weight along
the cycle, excluding both a closed path of decreasing reaches and a return to
a small deviation threshold.
   For pure client assignments, we use the repair operation of Vos [3, Lem-
ma 29]. Our overlap argument builds on the analysis in [3, Section 8.3]; the
proof below treats every cycle length and supplies the required mixed client
equilibria. The squared-load potential establishes termination of local client
repairs. To obtain the polynomial-time facility construction, we additionally
bound the number of exchanges in the mixed-equilibrium algorithm.

                     2. The golden-ratio theorem
   We first construct the client equilibria needed at a pair of locations. We
then show that the resulting finite menus contain a placement satisfying
both facilities’ deviation bounds. The main theorem follows by choosing the
client continuations from these menus.
2.1. Local equilibria and repair. A pure improvement moves weight w
from a facility of load H to one of load L < H precisely when w < H − L.
Both resulting loads lie strictly between L and H. This elementary fact gives
the following form of the repair from [3, Lemma 29].
Lemma 2.1. Suppose a pure client assignment has loads L < H, and each
common client initially on the lower-load side has weight at least H − L.
Repeatedly move a largest strictly improving client from the original higher-
load side to the original lower-load side, stopping when no such client remains
or when the load order first reverses. The output is an exact pure client Nash
equilibrium. Both final loads are at least L, and the original lower-load side
finishes strictly below H. At most k clients move.
Proof. Before a reversal, the load gap decreases, and the moved weights are
nonincreasing: a client too heavy to improve cannot become improving as
that gap decreases. If the order reverses after moving weight w, the new
gap is less than w. Every previously moved client and every common client
initially on the lower side has weight at least w, so none can move back.
The remaining clients are on the new lower side. If no reversal occurs, the
stopping condition and the initial weight bound give the same conclusion.
Each move leaves both loads in the preceding load interval and each client
moves at most once.                                                      □
   In particular, putting every common client on one side always gives either
an equilibrium or a seed to which Lemma 2.1 applies. Thus every placement
has a pure default continuation.
   For comparable reaches and sufficiently small common clients, the next
lemma gives one equilibrium in which both facilities receive a prescribed
fraction of the opposite reach. We construct it by assigning an individual
mixing probability to each common client.

Theorem 2.5. Every instance of Definition 1.1 has a ϕ-approximate subgame-
perfect equilibrium. With n positive binary-rational client weights and
N explicitly listed locations, one can construct such an equilibrium with
O(N 2 (n + 1)4 ) rational operations and polynomial intermediate bit length.
The output gives rational on-path and unilateral-deviation continuations and
a polynomial-time default rule for every other placement.

Proof. Choose (s, t), e by Lemma 2.4, and after each unilateral deviation
select a menu member minimizing the deviator’s load. By (2.2) and (2.3), the
deviating load is at most ϕ times the corresponding on-path load. Since the
two deviation families consist of disjoint ordered placements, these selections,
completed elsewhere by Lemma 2.1, define a complete continuation rule. The
menu bounds below give the stated complexity.                                 □

   Krogmann et al. [2, Theorem 5] give two-facility instances with no α-
approximate equilibrium for any prescribed α < ϕ. Thus the existence factor
in Theorem 2.5 is best possible. Their unrestricted instances lie within the
common-catalog class. Their decision hardness result allows varying facility
numbers and distinct location restrictions; it is a different statement from
the two-facility construction proved here.

2.4. The constructive bound. Construct all pair menus, store one member
attaining each minimum u(s, t), and compute d by (2.2). Scan the menu
members for (2.3); Lemma 2.4 guarantees success. Return the selected
member and the minimizing continuations for the at most 2N − 2 actual
unilateral deviations. At all other placements, use the all-common-client
seed and Lemma 2.1.
   At a pair with k common customers P has at most 2(k + 1) outputs and
costs O((k + 1)3 ) rational operations: each guarded repair moves at most
k customers and a direct scan per move costs O(k + 1). T has O((k + 1)2 )
outputs and costs O((k + 1)3 ) including direct Nash verification. E is
linear. Appendix C proves an O((k + 1)4 ) bound for C. Therefore all menus,
minima, maxima, cycle selection and scanning require O(N 2 (n + 1)4 ) rational
operations. Explicitly storing every probability vector uses O(N 2 (n + 1)3 )
rational entries, still polynomial.
   Every pure load is a sum of input weights. Every exchange coordinate
in Appendix C is an input endpoint or a signed sum of input weights and
the slice target. The later mixer gaps divide such sums by an integer at
most n, and probabilities additionally divide by an input weight. Thus all
intermediate rational encodings have length polynomial in the total binary
input length. Comparisons with q are exact: for rational z ≥ 0, the sign of
z − q is the sign of z 2 + z − 1, and z < 0 is immediate. Using q 2 = 1 − q
reduces all other irrational thresholds to this form. Zero-payoff cases are
handled by cross-multiplication, without division by zero. This proves the
stated bit complexity.

B.6. Termination of pure improvements. If a shared client of weight
w strictly improves by leaving a facility of load H for one of load L < H,
then w < H − L. The new loads H − w, L + w both lie strictly between
L, H. The absolute load gap strictly decreases; if its sign reverses, the new
absolute gap is less than w. The potential L2A + L2B strictly decreases, so
any sequence of strict pure improvements terminates in this finite game. All
constructions and repairs above therefore use ordinary independent mixed

is to obtain the exchange inequalities used in Appendix B.3. Computing a
global maximum of a convex quadratic function is unnecessary.

Theorem C.1. This algorithm terminates after O((m + 1)3 ) moves and
O((m + 1)4 ) rational arithmetic operations. Its output is a vertex at which
no two-coordinate endpoint move increases F .
Proof. If a nonterminal move changes the pivot from weight z to weight v,
the old pivot reaches the bound sz. The gain is
                                2(z − sd)(z − v).
Since |d| < z, a strict gain implies v < z. Every pivot change strictly
decreases its weight, so there are at most m − 1 changes.

and |d′ | > |d|. Fix a maximal phase with one pivot. When such a flip reverses

the nonzero sign of d, it requires v > |d| and leaves |d′ | = 2v − |d| > v. The
weights of successive sign-reversing flips therefore strictly increase. There
are at most m − 1 reversals in one phase. Between reversals every nonpivot

aligned with the sign of d, and afterwards that coordinate is opposed to that
sign. A first flip from d = 0 merely establishes a sign; a strict gain never

most m phases and at most one final all-bound move. Scanning at most
m − 1 pairs for each move proves the claimed operation bound. The estimate
is independent of weight magnitudes and separations.                         □

C.2. The exact inequalities supplied by the output. Suppose the
returned vertex still has pivot weight z and value d. On each pair segment, F
is convex. Since the opposite endpoint has no larger value, no interior point
has larger value either. Its directional derivative at the current endpoint
therefore gives
(C.1)            zi = +v ⇒ d ≤ v,        zi = −v ⇒ d ≥ −v.
For d > 0 the endpoint comparisons also imply
(C.2)         zi = +v ⇒ v ≥ z,        zi = −v, v < z ⇒ v ≤ d.
Indeed, for a positive coordinate, if 2v ≥ z − d, moving the pivot to z has
gain 2(z − d)(z − v); otherwise flipping the coordinate has gain 4v(d + v) > 0.
Either alternative excludes v < z. For a negative coordinate with v < z,
if 2v ≥ z + d, moving the pivot to −z has the forbidden positive gain
2(z + d)(z − v). Otherwise its full flip has gain 4v(v − d), forcing v ≤ d. For
d < 0 the signs reverse: pure negative coordinates have weight at least z,
and pure positive coordinates below z have weight at most −d.
   These are exactly the local derivative and four endpoint comparisons in
Appendix B.3. That appendix proves the initial customer equilibrium using
(C.1). Its positive-gap branch uses the two statements of (C.2); its negative-
gap branch uses their sign reversal. The later mass inequalities, explicit
mixer choices and pure repairs never compare quadratic objectives again.
Thus every remaining argument in Appendix B is valid for this polynomially
obtained vertex.

target −10, the vertex (14, −5, −19) has no improving pair endpoint, but its
value 582 is below the value 654 at (−14, −13, 17). This distinction is why
only the required exchange inequalities are claimed.

proved there and is preserved throughout.
   For completeness, this pure improvement rule moves at most k customers
in a two-facility game with k movable common customers, even with private
base loads. An improving weight w changes absolute gap g to |g − 2w| < g.
Before a sign reversal, candidates on the higher side can only disappear, so
chosen weights are nonincreasing. At a reversal the new gap is below the last
moved weight; every earlier moved customer is therefore permanently stable,
as all future gaps are smaller. Any newly movable customer has weight
below the new gap, hence below the preceding chosen weight. Inductively no
customer moves twice. A direct scan for each move costs O((k + 1)2 ) rational
operations. Fixed customers are simply absorbed into the private base loads
in this argument.
   The exchange algorithm consequently dominates the running time: O((k +
1)4 ) rational operations per strong chord. Every exchange coordinate is an
endpoint or the slice target minus a signed sum of input weights. Subsequent
mixed gaps divide signed sums by integers at most k, and probabilities divide
by the corresponding input weight. The encoding lengths remain polynomial.
The exact golden-ratio comparisons described in Section 2.4 avoid numerical
tolerances.
   The elementary two-facility improvement analysis above is also consistent
with the maximum-weight improvement rule studied for load balancing by
Even-Dar, Kesselman, and Mansour [1]. The box-slice exchange bound is
supplied in full here; no efficient global convex-maximization claim is used.
