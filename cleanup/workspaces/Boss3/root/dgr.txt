                                                                                          Abstract
                                                   We study bicriteria versions of Makespan Minimization on Unrelated Machines and Santa
                                               Claus by allowing a constrained number of rejections. Given an instance of Makespan Minimiza-
                                               tion on Unrelated Machines where the optimal makespan for scheduling n jobs on m unrelated
                                               machines is T , (Feige and Vondrák, 2006) gave an algorithm that schedules a (1 − 1/e + 10−180 )
                                               fraction of jobs in time T . We show the ratio can be improved to 0.6533 > 1 − 1/e + 0.02 if we
                                               allow makespan 3T /2. To the best our knowledge, this is the first result examining the tradeoff
                                               between makespan and the fraction of scheduled jobs when the makespan is not T or 2T .
                                                   For the Santa Claus problem (the “Max-Min” version of Makespan Minimization), the anal-
                                               ogous bicriteria objective was studied by (Golovin, 2005), who gave an algorithm providing an
                                               allocation so a (1 − 1/k) fraction of agents receive value at least T /k, for any k ∈ Z+ and T
                                               being the optimal minimum value every agent can receive. We provide the first hardness result
                                               by showing there are constants δ, ε > 0 such that it is NP-hard to find an allocation where
                                               a (1 − δ) fraction of agents receive value at least (1 − ε)T . To prove this hardness result, we
                                               introduce a bicriteria version of Set Packing, which may be of independent interest, and prove
                                               some algorithmic and hardness results for it. Overall, we believe these bicriteria scheduling
                                               problems warrant further study as they provide an interesting lens to understand how robust
                                               the difficulty of the original optimization goal might be.


                                               1    Introduction
                                                   Two of the most central problems in scheduling theory are Makespan Minimization on Un-
                                               related Machines and its dual, the Santa Claus problem (also called Max-Min Fair Allocation).
                                               We refer to the two problems as Makespan and Santa Claus, in short. In Makespan, a set
                                               of jobs J are available to schedule on a set of machines M , where the processing time of job
                                               j ∈ J on machine i ∈ M is pi,j . We let pi,j = ∞ if job j cannot be processed on machine i.
                                               The jobs must be assigned non-preemptively, and the goal is to minimize the makespan, i.e.,
                                               the time the last machine finishes processing. In Santa Claus, a set of items I are available
                                               to assign to a set of agents A, where the value agent i ∈ A receives from item j ∈ I is pi,j ≥ 0.
                                               Each item can be allocated to at most one agent, and the total value agent i receives is the sum
                                               of the values from individual items. The goal is to find an allocation of items to agents so that
                                               the minimum total value of any agent is maximized.
                                                   For Makespan, the seminal work of Lenstra, Shmoys, and Tardos gave a factor 2 approx-
                                               imation algorithm and showed that the problem is NP-hard to approximate within a factor

          For both problems in their most general versions, progress on the hardness and algorithmic
      fronts has been stuck. On the hardness side, e.g., for Santa Claus, all natural approaches via
      local gadgets break down when one can allocate half the intended value to agents. Specifically,
      [BCG09] showed the local gadgets used in all previous reductions can be modified to have
      degree two, i.e., each item is shared by at most two agents. However, [CCK09] gave a (2 + ε)-
      approximation algorithm for this special case, ruling out the hope to get better hardness factor
      using only such gadgets. On the algorithmic front, the lack of progress is largely due to the
      absence of good convex programs for either problem. For instance,      √ the configuration linear
      program has an integrality gap of 2 for Makespan [VW14] and Ω( n) for Santa Claus, for
      n the number of agents1 .
          Given the difficulty in improving algorithms or hardness in the general setting, much work
      over the past several decades has focused on breaking these barriers in special cases. The notable
      cases for Santa Claus include the restricted setting [Fei08,AKS17,CM18,CM19,DRZ20,HS23],
      unrelated graph balancing [BCG09,CCK09,VW14], and Max-Min Degree Arborescence [BCG09,
      BR23, Bam25]. For Makespan, some of the important cases that have been studied include
      the restricted setting [Sve11, HO15, JR17], graph balancing [EKS14, VW14, JR19], and in the
      fixed-parameter tractable regime [MW15]. These special settings are valuable because they help
      the community identify which aspects of the problems really contribute to their difficulty. For
      example, one such insight was suggested by Verschae and Wiese [VW14] for Makespan: the
      configuration linear program has integrality gap 1.833 < 2 in the restricted setting, but has
      gap 2 for unrelated graph balancing, thus hinting that perhaps the difficulty in the problem is
      present in unrelated graph balancing but not in the restricted setting2 . On the other hand, the
      unrelated graph balancing setting is much easier to handle for Santa Claus, as there exist
      several constant factor approximations [BCG09,CCK09], indicating that perhaps the difficulties
      for both problems are not even the same.
          In this paper, we study a different type of relaxation for these problems. For Makespan
      we allow a 1 − α fraction of jobs to be rejected (i.e., not scheduled on any machine), for Santa
      Claus we allow a 1 − α fraction of agents to be rejected (i.e., receive 0 value). Then we study
      how this affects the approximation factor β for makespan/value guarantee. While a standard
      approximation algorithm fixes α = 1 and optimizes β, we study the trade-off between the two
      parameters.
          This relaxation of Makespan is the unweighted case of the Maximum General Assignment
      Problem [FGMS06], for which Feige and Vondrák [FV06] gave an algorithm that achieved α =
      1 − 1/e + 10−180 and β = 1. On the other hand, Nutov, Beniaminy and Yuster [NBY06] showed
      the NP-hardness of α = 1 − ε and β = 1 for some ε > 0. We remark that [FV06]’s improvement
      over 1 − 1/e relies on a delicate hierarchy of parameters that has to be carefully balanced, which
      makes it difficult to turn the 10−180 into a larger constant. In this work, we give an algorithm
      that reaches α > 1 − 1/e + 0.02 and β = 3/2.

    We highlight that the optimal value T we compare against is the optimal makespan for
scheduling all jobs, not the (potentially smaller) optimal makespan of scheduling a subset. We
also remark that scheduling all jobs in makespan less than 3T /2 is NP-hard [LST90], so when
initiating the study of the trade-off between α and 1 < β < 2, it is natural to consider β = 3/2.
    Allowing some jobs to be rejected is not only a natural variant, but we believe it still captures
much of the difficulty in the original problems. We conjecture there are some “transition points”
on the curve between α and β, where when one wishes to schedule a slightly larger fraction of
jobs (say from α to α + δ, for a small constant δ > 0), a large jump in the approximation
factor for the makespan, β, is needed. One regime where it seems difficult to smooth the curve
is when β = 1; it is not clear whether increasing the approximation factor on the makespan
slightly allows us to schedule a larger fraction of jobs. Similarly, points near (α, β) = (1, 2) are
interesting because it seems challenging to design an algorithm that rejects a small constant ε
(say ε < 1/10) fraction of jobs and schedules the rest in makespan (2 − ε) · T .
    Santa Claus with constrained rejections has been studied algorithmically by Golovin
[Gol05], who gave an algorithm which for any k ∈ Z+ , finds an allocation for Santa Claus
where at least a (1 − 1/k) fraction of agents obtain value at least 1/k · T . In this work, we show
the hardness of this problem, although in the high-value regime.
Theorem 2. For the Santa Claus problem, there are universal constants δ, ε > 0, such that
given the existence of an assignment where all n agents receive total value at least T , it’s NP-hard
to find an assignment where (1 − δ) · n agents receive total value (1 − ε) · T .
    To prove Theorem 2, we introduce bicriteria Set Packing as an intermediate problem (see
Definition 3). In the traditional Set Packing problem, we are given as input a collection S of
sets over a universe U of elements, and the goal is to find the maximum number of disjoint sets
from S. In bicriteria Set Packing, instead of being forced to choose a whole set from S as in
the original formulation, we relax the problem to finding large disjoint subsets of the sets in S.
    While we motivated bicriteria Makespan and Santa Claus from the long-standing gaps
between hardness and approximations, bicriteria Set Packing is itself an interesting combina-
torial optimization problem due to the tight hardness results for Set Packing (see Section 1.1).
In other words, we know Set Packing is a hard problem, but our understanding of how rigid
the hardness is to relaxing some of the constraints is limited. In this vein, we obtain the following
algorithmic and hardness results.
Theorem 3. For the Set Packing problem, for any δ > 0, there exists ε > 0 and a polynomial-
time randomized algorithm, which given as input a collection S of n sets over a universe U and
the existence of m sets in S that form a partition of U , finds m′ = (1 − δ) · m many sets
S1 , . . . , Sm′ together with their subsets A1 ⊆ S1 , . . . , Am′ ⊆ Sm′ , such that for every i ∈ [m′ ],
|Ai | ≥ ε · |Si |, and the sets A1 , . . . , Am′ are disjoint.

Theorem 4. For the Set Packing problem, there are universal constants ε > 0, α > β > 0,
such that given as input a collection S of n sets over a universe U and the existence of m = α · n
sets in S that form a partition of U , it is NP-hard to find m′ = β · n many sets S1 , . . . , Sm′
in S together with their subsets A1 ⊆ S1 , . . . , Am′ ⊆ Sm′ , such that for every i ∈ [m′ ], |Ai | ≥
(1 − ε) · |Si |, and the sets A1 , . . . , Am′ are disjoint.
    We note that in bicriteria Set Packing, we assume the existence of m sets partitioning U ,
while in general Set Packing, this might not be the case.
    We give the algorithm for bicriteria Makespan (Theorem 1) in Section 2. We show the
hardness for bicriteria Santa Claus (Theorem 2) in Section 3. The algorithm and the hardness
for bicriteria Set Packing (Theorem 3 and Theorem 4) are in Section 4 and Section 5.

    For the classic problem of minimizing the makespan on unrelated machines, it is a long-
standing open problem in scheduling theory to develop a polynomial time algorithm that beats
the 2-approximation [LST90]. Several lines of work explore additional assumptions on the input
that make it easier to break the boundary of 2, including in the FPT setting [HS76,JP99,MW15]
and in graph balancing [EKS14, HO15, JR19]. Algorithms in the restricted assignment setting
(pi,j ∈ {∞, pj }) estimate the optimal makespan within factors strictly less than 2 but do not
necessarily terminate in polynomial time [Sve11, JR17].
    Santa Claus was first introduced in the job-machine scheduling setting [Woe97, ES99].
The configuration LP was introduced for Santa Claus by Bansal       √ and Sviridenko [BS06].
Asadpour and Saberi prove √that the CLP has integrality gap Ω( n) and round the solution
to the CLP to obtain a Õ( n) approximation algorithm [AS07], before the improvement to
a Õ(mε )-approximation algorithm with running time mO(1/ε) , for ε = Ω(log log m/ log m), by
Chakrabarty, Chuzhoy, and Khanna [CCK09]. Recently, Bamas et al. showed that for any
α ≥ 2, ε > 0, a polynomial-time (2 − 1/α)-approximation for Makespan leads to a polynomial-
time (α + ε) approximation for Santa Claus, and under the two-value restriction, they are
actually equivalent [BLM+ 24].
    For the bicriteria Makespan problem, Chekuri and Khanna [CK05] observed that the algo-
rithm of Lenstra, Shmoys, and Tardos already schedules at least a 1/2 of the jobs in makespan T .
In the case when β = 1, bicriteria Makespan is a special case of the Maximum General Assign-
ment Problem. Previous work on the Maximum General Assignment Problem by Fleischer, Goe-
mans, Mirrokni, and Sviridenko [FGMS06] proves one can schedule a 1 − 1/e fraction of jobs in
makespan T . Feige and Vondrák [FV06] improved the fraction to 1−1/e+10−180 . The best NP-
inapproximability ratio of the Maximum General Assignment Problem is 10/11 by Chakrabarty
and Goel [CG10]. However, in their construction, the jobs are weighted, which makes it inca-
pable to fit into our setting. It was proven by Nutov, Beniaminy, and Yuster [NBY06] that the
unit weight case (a.k.a. maximizing the number of jobs scheduled) is also APX-hard.
    For bicriteria Santa Claus, Golovin proposed an algorithm which for any k ∈ Z+ , finds an
allocation for Santa Claus where a (1 − 1/k) fraction of agents obtain value 1/k · T [Gol05].

1.2    Preliminaries
    We begin with preliminaries for Makespan. It is helpful to consider the bipartite graph
G = (V, E) where V = M ∪J  ˙ and E is the set of (machine, job) pairs (i, j) where pi,j < ∞.
A schedule O is simply a subset of E. T denotes the optimal makespan. In our algorithms
for (α, β)-bicriteria Makespan, we partition E into disjoint sets of large and small edges, EL
and ES , according to the processing times EL = {(i, j) | i ∈ M, j ∈ J, T2 < pi,j ≤ T }, and
ES = {(i, j) | i ∈ M, j ∈ J, 0 ≤pi,j ≤ T2 }, and define GL and GS to be the bipartite graphs
induced by EL and ES , respectively. We let m⋆ denote the size of a maximum matching in GL .
When we input an instance of Makespan into an algorithm for (α, β)-bicriteria Makespan,
we denote the input with the shorthand Γ = (J, M, T, E ′ ), for J the jobs, M the machines, T
the optimal makespan, and E ′ ⊆ E the edges along which we make assignments.
Definition 1 (Makespan Problem). In the Makespan problem, we are given as input a set
of m machines M , a set of n jobs J, and a table {pi,j }i∈M,j∈J , where pi,j is the processing time
of job j on machine i. We write pi,j = ∞ if job j cannot be scheduled on machine i. The goal
is to assign jobs to machines, such that the maximum total processing time on any machine is
minimized.
    For 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 ≤ β ≤ 2, (α, β)-bicriteria Makespan is the task where given the optimal
makespan T , the goal is to schedule a subset of α · n jobs on M within makespan β · T .
    The definitions for Santa Claus and Set Packing and their bicriteria variants are as
follows. Other notations are self-contained in those relevant sections.
Definition 2 (Santa Claus Problem). In the Santa Claus problem, we are given as input
a set of n agents A, a set of m items I, and a table {pi,j }i∈A,j∈J where pi,j is the value agent
i can get on receiving item j. The goal is to assign items to agents to maximize the minimum
total value of any agent.
    For 0 ≤ α, β ≤ 1, (α, β)-bicriteria Santa Claus is the task where given the optimum value
T , the goal is to allocate items to agents so that at least α · n agents have total value at least
β · T.
Definition 3 (Set Packing Problem). In the Set Packing problem, we are given as input a
collection of sets S over a universe U , and the goal is to find the maximum number of disjoint
sets in S.
    For 0 ≤ α, β ≤ 1, (α, β)-bicriteria Set Packing is the task where given the guarantee that
there are m sets in S partitioning U , the goal is to find m′ = α · m many sets S1 , . . . , Sm′ in S
together with their subsets A1 ⊆ S1 , . . . , Am′ ⊆ Sm′ , such that for every i ∈ [m′ ], |Ai | ≥ β · |Si |,
and the sets A1 , . . . , Am′ are disjoint.

6     Conclusions
    In this paper, we studied the bicriteria versions of several important scheduling problems,
namely Makespan, Santa Claus, and Set Packing.
    For Makespan, we provided a bicriteria approximation algorithm: we can schedule more
than 0.6533 fraction of the jobs within makespan (3/2) · T , where T is the optimal makespan
to schedule all jobs. We suggest as future work to prove hardness or algorithmic results for
scheduling a (1 − 1/e + ε) fraction of jobs in makespan (3/2 − δ) · T , for ε > 0.02 and δ being
a small constant. Another trade-off point that would be interesting future work is scheduling a
(1 − ε) fraction of jobs in makespan (2 − δ) · T , for ε, δ small constants.
    For Santa Claus, we provided hardness results with a constant fraction of agent rejections.
An interesting open question would be closing the ε vs. (1 − ε) gap between the algorithm
in [Gol05] and the hardness from us on the value each agent receives.
    As a by-product, for Set Packing, we provided the hardness for (1 − δ0 , 1 − ε)-bicriteria
for some δ0 , ε > 0, and gave an algorithm for (1 − δ, ε)-bicriteria where δ can be arbitrarily close
to 0. We conjecture ε = 1/2 might be the threshold of going from easy to hard, since when
ε < 1/2, it’s possible for a pair of sets to both pick an ε-fraction so that the subsets are disjoint,
even if the two sets are identical. Thus, we suggest as future work to study the case when ε is
around 1/2.
